Why Does Anxiety Get Worse the Day After Drinking Alcohol?

September 25, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Alcohol calms the brain while you drink, and the brain pushes back to stay in balance. When the alcohol wears off, that pushback is left unopposed, so you feel more restless and anxious than before you started. Broken sleep, mild dehydration, and the other effects of a hangover add to it. People who are shy or anxious to begin with seem to feel this next-day anxiety, often called "hangxiety," more than others.

What is "hangxiety," and is it a real thing?

"Hangxiety" is a popular name, not a diagnosis. But the symptom behind it is well documented. The National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA) lists anxiety and irritability among the typical symptoms in its hangover fact sheet, alongside fatigue, headache, nausea, sweating, and raised blood pressure.

NIAAA's clinical guide for health professionals goes further. It says that anxiety-like symptoms can occur after a single heavy drinking episode, even in people who do not have an anxiety disorder. It notes that the press calls this "hangxiety." Those symptoms can build between drinking episodes and reach high levels during alcohol withdrawal.

NIAAA also says hangover symptoms peak when blood alcohol returns to about zero, and can last 24 hours or longer.

What happens in the brain when the alcohol wears off?

Alcohol acts on the brain's two main balancing systems. GABA is the main "brake" chemical, which quiets nerve cells. Glutamate is the main "accelerator," which excites them.

A review in the NIAAA journal Alcohol Health and Research World explains the pattern. Alcohol adds to GABA's calming effect at GABA-A receptors, an effect it compares to benzodiazepine tranquilizers. Alcohol also blocks the NMDA receptor, a glutamate receptor that is very sensitive to alcohol. The brain responds to both changes by adjusting to restore balance.

  • While you drink: more braking, less accelerating. Many people feel relaxed.
  • As alcohol leaves: the brain's counter-adjustments remain for a while. The system tips toward excitation.

The strongest evidence for this rebound comes from long-term heavy drinking, where the brain's adjustments are large. A 2014 review of alcohol withdrawal describes nervous system hyperexcitability, autonomic arousal, and negative mood, driven by changes in glutamate, GABA, and other systems. NIAAA describes a milder version after a single night as a "mini-withdrawal". The brain quickly adjusts to alcohol's calming effect, so people can feel more restless and anxious once the buzz fades.

What else makes you feel anxious the next day?

The brain rebound is only part of it. NIAAA lists several other contributors to hangover symptoms:

  • Disrupted sleep. People fall asleep faster after drinking, but sleep is fragmented and they wake earlier.
  • Mild dehydration. Alcohol suppresses vasopressin, a hormone that tells the kidneys to hold on to water. You urinate more and lose fluid.
  • Inflammation and acetaldehyde. Alcohol raises inflammation, and its toxic byproduct, acetaldehyde, adds to it.
  • Stomach irritation. Alcohol irritates the stomach lining and increases acid, which causes nausea.

Sleep deserves a closer look. A review of all known studies on alcohol and sleep in healthy volunteers found a consistent pattern at every dose. Sleep came faster and was deeper in the first half of the night, then more disrupted in the second half. At moderate and high doses, most studies found less total REM sleep. Poor sleep and anxiety feed each other, as our guide to why anxiety keeps you awake explains. If broken sleep is a pattern for you, insomnia.md covers it in more detail.

Low blood sugar is sometimes blamed too, but NIAAA's current hangover fact sheet does not list it among the causes. The same fact sheet says research has not linked the size of electrolyte changes to how bad a hangover is.

Who is most likely to get hangxiety?

People who are shy or anxious appear to be hit harder. A 2019 study in Personality and Individual Differences tested 97 social drinkers at home. Fifty drank as they normally would and 47 stayed sober. In highly shy people, drinking slightly lowered anxiety that evening. The next day, their anxiety rose significantly. In that highly shy group, the size of the next-day rise tracked with higher scores on the AUDIT, a screening test for alcohol use disorder. The authors suggested hangover anxiety could be an early marker of risk in shy drinkers.

Other studies point the same way, with caveats:

  • A 2023 survey of 5,111 university students in the Netherlands and the U.K. found that people who get hangovers had higher baseline anxiety and stress than people who say they don't. But the gap was less than 1 point on a 42-point scale, which the authors said is unlikely to be clinically meaningful.
  • A 2026 study of 334 young adult drinkers in Canada looked at anxiety sensitivity, the fear of anxious feelings. Only the cognitive part of it predicted more hangover-related anxiety a month later.

How are alcohol use and anxiety disorders connected?

The two often travel together. NIAAA says 20% to 40% of people treated for anxiety disorders also have alcohol use disorder. The anxiety disorders that most often co-occur with it are generalized anxiety disorder, social anxiety disorder, and panic disorder.

Part of the reason is simple. Alcohol is easy to get, and many people use it to cope with anxiety. NIAAA warns that alcohol may seem to relieve anxiety in the short term. Over time, though, heavy drinking and repeated withdrawal can make both the anxiety and the drinking worse. A review in Alcohol Research & Health adds that withdrawal-related anxiety is one factor that drives relapse in people who are dependent on alcohol.

This creates a trap. NIAAA says a morning drink may briefly ease some symptoms but can prolong the hangover.

Clinicians often try to sort out which came first. NIAAA suggests building a timeline: when anxiety started, when drinking started, and whether anxiety lifts during long breaks from alcohol. Anxiety that fades with abstinence points to alcohol as a cause. Anxiety that persists suggests a separate anxiety disorder that needs its own treatment.

When is next-day anxiety a warning sign?

An occasional anxious morning after a heavy night is common. These patterns deserve a closer look:

  • You regularly feel anxious or shaky the day after drinking.
  • You drink to calm down, or you drink again to ease the morning after.
  • You feel anxious between drinking days, not just after them.
  • You are drinking more over time to get the same effect.

Pay attention to physical signs, too. MedlinePlus says alcohol withdrawal can follow when a person who drinks heavily on a regular basis suddenly stops. Symptoms often start within 8 hours of the last drink and tend to peak by 24 to 72 hours. They include anxiety, shakiness, sweating, rapid heart rate, and insomnia. A severe form, delirium tremens, can cause confusion, hallucinations, and seizures. If you drink heavily every day, talk to a doctor before stopping. Withdrawal may need medical care.

For confidential help finding treatment, call SAMHSA's National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357. It is free and open 24 hours a day, every day of the year. If you are in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, call or text the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. Our guide on when to see a doctor about anxiety covers other signs that call for help.

The bottom line

Next-day anxiety after drinking is real and has a clear cause. Alcohol quiets the brain, the brain pushes back, and the pushback lingers after the alcohol is gone. Broken sleep and other hangover effects pile on. Shy and anxious people seem most affected, and for them hangxiety may be an early sign of risky drinking. NIAAA says no remedy has been proven to prevent a hangover; drinking less, or not drinking, is the only sure way to avoid one. If the anxious mornings are frequent, or you find yourself drinking to manage anxiety, that pattern is worth raising with a doctor.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does hangxiety last?

NIAAA says hangover symptoms peak when blood alcohol returns to about zero and can last 24 hours or longer. Anxiety from a single night usually eases as the rest of the hangover does. Anxiety that lasts for days or shows up between drinking episodes is worth discussing with a doctor.

Does drinking in the morning help hangover anxiety?

It may blunt some symptoms for a short time, but NIAAA says it can prolong the hangover. Drinking to ease the morning after is also one of the patterns linked to problem drinking. It tends to deepen the cycle rather than break it.

Can a hangover cause a panic attack?

Hangover symptoms such as sweating, nausea, and raised blood pressure overlap with the physical side of panic. For people prone to panic, those sensations can trigger fear and escalate. Panic disorder is one of the anxiety disorders that most often co-occurs with alcohol use disorder, according to NIAAA.

Is there anything that prevents hangxiety?

NIAAA says no hangover remedy has been proven to work, and only time lets the body recover. Research has not linked electrolyte changes to hangover severity. Drinking less, or not drinking, is the only reliable way to avoid a hangover and the anxiety that comes with it.

Should I worry if I only get anxious after drinking?

Not necessarily, since anxiety after a heavy night is a common hangover symptom. But in one study of shy drinkers, bigger next-day anxiety rises tracked with higher scores on an alcohol use disorder screening test. If it happens often, it is a good reason to look at how much and why you drink.
