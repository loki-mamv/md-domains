Can Anxiety Cause Muscle Twitching All Over Your Body?

November 20, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Yes. Stress and anxiety are common triggers for benign muscle twitches, which MedlinePlus (reviewed January 2026) calls the most common type of twitching. They often hit the eyelids, calves, or thumbs and can jump around the body. Twitching with no weakness or muscle loss is very rarely serious.

Why does anxiety make your muscles twitch?

A twitch, or fasciculation, is a small, brief contraction of a few muscle fibers. The fibers are all served by a single motor nerve fiber. You can often see the skin flicker, but the twitch does not move the joint.

MedlinePlus lists several everyday triggers for benign twitches:

  • Stress and anxiety
  • Lack of sleep
  • Caffeine, amphetamines, and other stimulants
  • Some medicines, such as diuretics, corticosteroids, and estrogens
  • Recent exercise
  • Low potassium and other metabolic problems

Anxiety tends to stack these triggers. You sleep less, drink more coffee to cope, and stay tense all day. Fast breathing during anxiety can add to it. MedlinePlus explains that overbreathing lowers carbon dioxide in the blood. That can cause muscle spasms in the hands and feet, plus tingling and numbness around the mouth and arms.

Twitches are also more common in healthy people than most realize. In a 1998 study of 122 healthy volunteers, sensors picked up fasciculation potentials in the small foot and hand muscles of 77% of them. The count rose with scores on an anxiety rating scale.

What is benign fasciculation syndrome?

Benign fasciculation syndrome (BFS) is the name doctors use when twitching is frequent and lasts weeks or months, but the nerve exam is normal. Many people with BFS also report cramps, tingling, or a feeling of weakness that does not show up on testing.

The link to anxiety is real but not simple. In a 2018 prospective study, researchers followed 35 people with BFS for 24 months. Most said they felt anxious about the twitching. But only 14% scored as anxious on a standard anxiety scale. Twitching was most common in the calves. It persisted in 93% of people, and anxiety levels did not change over time.

So anxiety is one trigger, and worry about the twitches is very common. BFS also happens in calm people. In both cases, the twitch itself is not dangerous.

Health care workers show up often in these studies. That fits a pattern: people who know what ALS is tend to notice twitches and fear the worst. If you notice that you scan your body for twitches many times a day, read about health anxiety.

Could twitching all over be a sign of ALS?

This is the fear that drives most searches. The studies that followed people with benign twitching are reassuring:

  • A Mayo Clinic study found 121 patients with benign fasciculations, a normal exam, and normal nerve tests. Follow-up came 2 to 32 years after diagnosis. None developed symptomatic motor neuron disease.
  • A 2021 Lisbon follow-up study looked at 37 people referred for fear of ALS. Of the 24 followed (median 4.7 years), about two-thirds reported improvement. Their nerve test results stayed stable.
  • A 2024 systematic review pooled 3 studies with 180 BFS patients. No patient developed motor neuron disease at follow-up. Twitching persisted in 98.3%, improved in 51.7%, and worsened in 4.1%.

Two honest caveats apply. These are small studies, so they cannot give an exact risk figure. The review also notes that rare case reports describe BFS that later turned into motor neuron disease. What the data do show is that twitching alone, with a normal exam, is not how ALS usually behaves.

The NINDS ALS page lists twitches as one early symptom. But it lists them with muscle weakness in an arm, a leg, or the neck, plus stiffness, slurred speech, and trouble chewing or swallowing. Symptoms most often start between ages 55 and 75. In ALS, weakness and muscle loss spread over time.

What signs mean you should see a doctor soon?

Doctors look past the twitch to what comes with it. Book a visit if twitching comes with any of these:

  • Real weakness: you trip on one foot, drop things, or cannot open jars you used to open. Feeling weak is common with anxiety. Failing a task is different.
  • Muscle loss: one hand, shoulder, or calf looks visibly thinner than the other side.
  • Speech or swallowing changes: slurred or nasal speech, choking, or trouble chewing.
  • Loss of feeling: a lasting change in sensation in one area, as opposed to brief tingling.
  • Twitching that lasts: MedlinePlus advises contacting a provider for long-term or persistent twitches.

Twitching that moves around the body is usually less worrying than twitching locked in one weak muscle. Twitching that is worse after coffee, poor sleep, or a hard workout also points to a benign cause.

What tests do doctors usually order for twitching?

The most useful test is a careful exam of strength, reflexes, and muscle bulk. Your provider will also ask when the twitching started, where it happens, and what else you feel.

According to MedlinePlus, tests depend on the suspected cause and may include:

  • Blood tests for electrolytes, thyroid function, and blood chemistry
  • Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies
  • MRI or CT of the brain or spine

Many people with twitching and a normal exam need only basic blood work. MedlinePlus lists thyroid function among the first blood tests, and thyroid problems can also cause anxiety symptoms. thyroid.md covers those symptoms. EMG is usually saved for people with weakness, muscle loss, or abnormal reflexes. If you do get one, ask the neurologist to explain the result in plain terms. Keep the report so you do not need to repeat it.

How do you calm the twitch, check, worry loop?

MedlinePlus says benign twitches usually need no treatment. The harder part is the cycle: a twitch triggers fear, fear raises tension, tension brings more twitches. Checking (searching online, testing your grip, watching your calf) brings a moment of relief. Then it makes the fear stronger. Here is a four-week plan built on the triggers and treatments above.

  1. Week 1, rule out and reset. See your provider once for an exam and basic blood work. Write down the result. Cut caffeine by half and aim for a fixed bedtime.
  2. Week 2, track triggers, not twitches. Once a day, note sleep, caffeine, exercise, and stress level. Do not log each twitch. Look for patterns, such as worse twitching after short nights.
  3. Week 3, cut the checking. Stop strength self-tests and symptom searches. When the urge hits, wait 10 minutes and use a slow breathing technique to stop overbreathing.
  4. Week 4, review. Ask yourself whether any real red flag has appeared. If not, the plan is working, even if the twitches have not stopped. If worry still takes over your day, book therapy.

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the best-studied option here. NIMH calls CBT the "gold standard" psychotherapy for generalized anxiety disorder. It teaches you to question fearful thoughts and drop habits like checking.

The bottom line

Anxiety, poor sleep, caffeine, and exercise can all cause muscle twitching anywhere in the body. In the follow-up studies above, people with benign twitching and a normal exam did not go on to develop motor neuron disease. The twitches often last for months or years, and many improve. Watch for weakness, muscle loss, and speech or swallowing changes. If none of those are present, focus on sleep, caffeine, and the checking habit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can anxiety cause twitching in many muscles at once?

Yes. Benign twitches often move around, showing up in the calves one day and the arms or eyelids the next. MedlinePlus lists stress and anxiety among the most common triggers. Twitching that moves around without weakness is usually benign.

How long does anxiety twitching last?

Single episodes of benign twitching often last a few days. In benign fasciculation syndrome, twitching can persist for months or years. In a 2018 study, it persisted in 93% of 35 patients over 24 months, but none developed motor neuron disease.

Does magnesium stop muscle twitching?

Low levels of some minerals can cause twitching, and a blood test can check electrolytes. If your levels are normal, ask your provider before you start a supplement for twitching.

Should I get an EMG if I am worried about ALS?

An EMG is a nerve and muscle test. In the follow-up studies cited above, people with a normal exam and benign twitching did not develop motor neuron disease. Ask your doctor whether your exam findings call for one.

Why do my twitches get worse when I rest?

Twitches are often easier to notice at rest, when the body is still and attention turns inward. MedlinePlus also lists recent exercise as a common trigger. Neither pattern is a red flag on its own.
