Why Does My Anxiety Get Worse the Week Before My Period?

The short answer: the week before a period falls in the luteal phase, when hormone levels shift and some brains react strongly to the change. That reaction can show up as premenstrual syndrome (PMS), as the more severe premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), or as a flare of an anxiety disorder you already have. The three look alike in that week. A daily symptom record kept over at least two cycles is how a clinician tells them apart, and the treatment depends on the answer.

What happens in your body the week before a period?

The luteal phase is the roughly two weeks between ovulation and the next period. PMS and PMDD both cause symptoms in that window (Cochrane review, 2024). The exact cause is not known. The Office on Women's Health says hormonal changes across the cycle may play a role, and that serotonin levels also change through the cycle, with some women more sensitive to those changes (Office on Women's Health).

Research on PMDD points to sensitivity, not to a simple hormone shortage. One review describes PMDD as a disorder of "suboptimal sensitivity" to neuroactive steroid hormones. It focuses on allopregnanolone, a steroid that boosts the action of the GABA-A receptor. The authors propose that the receptor responds poorly as allopregnanolone rises and falls (Hantsoo and Epperson, 2020, Neurobiology of Stress). A later review says the findings do not yet form a complete picture (Hantsoo and Payne, 2023).

Is it PMS, PMDD, or an anxiety disorder flaring?

They are three different things.

  • PMS. Physical and emotional symptoms that appear after ovulation and go away within a few days after the period starts. As many as three in four women say they get PMS symptoms at some point (Office on Women's Health).
  • PMDD. A more serious condition that causes severe irritability, depression, or anxiety in the week or two before a period. Symptoms can include feelings of tension or anxiety and panic attacks. It affects up to 5% of women of childbearing age, and a diagnosis needs five or more symptoms, including one mood-related symptom (Office on Women's Health).
  • Premenstrual exacerbation. The worsening of an existing disorder in the premenstrual phase. The difference from PMS and PMDD is that the symptoms of the existing disorder are present through the whole cycle. They do not clear after the period (Lin et al., 2024, Frontiers in Psychiatry).

If you feel well after your period and anxious only before it, that points to PMS or PMDD. If you are anxious most days and worse before your period, that points to an exacerbation of an anxiety disorder such as generalized anxiety disorder. Both can be true at once.

How common is premenstrual worsening of an anxiety disorder?

Nobody has a firm number, and the evidence is weaker for anxiety than for some other conditions. A systematic review of 35 prospective studies found clear evidence of symptom worsening around menstruation for panic disorder, depression, psychotic disorders, eating disorders, and borderline personality disorder. It found less consistent evidence for anxiety in general. The authors concluded that cycle hormones may worsen symptoms in a hormone-sensitive subgroup (Nolan and Hughes, 2022, Archives of Women's Mental Health).

A second review reached a similar verdict. Anxiety and stress appear to be raised across the luteal phase in general, but the authors found the pattern for panic disorder less consistent. They said data were too limited to draw conclusions for generalized anxiety disorder or social anxiety disorder (Handy et al., 2022, Harvard Review of Psychiatry).

How do doctors tell them apart?

By timing, recorded day by day. No physical exam or lab test can diagnose PMDD (MedlinePlus). A summary of the 2023 guideline from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists says the timing of symptoms is the most important element of diagnosis (American Family Physician, 2024).

The record has to be prospective, which means written down each day and not recalled later. Researchers treat that method as the gold standard for measuring symptoms across the cycle (Nolan and Hughes, 2022). The label for a birth control pill approved for PMDD says symptoms are "assessed prospectively over at least two menstrual cycles" (Yaz prescribing information, DailyMed). Premenstrual exacerbation needs the same tracking across at least two symptomatic cycles (Lin et al., 2024). One tool is the Daily Record of Severity of Problems, a daily rating form that was tested in two studies and found to be reliable and valid (Endicott et al., 2006).

  1. Rate your anxiety every evening on the same simple scale.
  2. Mark the first day of each period.
  3. Continue for at least two full cycles, then take the record to your appointment.

What treatments have evidence behind them?

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists guideline notes that many patients benefit from combining several treatments (ACOG Clinical Practice Guideline No. 7, 2023).

  • SSRIs. A Cochrane review of 34 randomized trials found that SSRIs probably reduce premenstrual symptoms, and probably work better taken every day than in the luteal phase only. Nausea, insomnia, and lower libido were more common than with placebo (Jespersen et al., 2024). Sertraline and controlled-release paroxetine list PMDD on their labels (Zoloft label, Paxil CR label). The sertraline label gives a recommended starting dosage of 50 mg per day in adult women with PMDD, taken either continuously or only during the luteal phase.
  • Birth control pills. A pill with drospirenone and ethinyl estradiol is indicated for PMDD symptoms in people who choose an oral contraceptive for contraception. Its label says effectiveness beyond three menstrual cycles has not been evaluated, and it carries a boxed warning about smoking and serious cardiovascular events (Yaz label).
  • Cognitive behavioral therapy. In a randomized trial of 174 women with PMDD, an 8-week therapist-guided online program reduced symptom intensity and impairment, and the gains held at 6 months (Weise et al., 2019).
  • Lifestyle changes. Regular aerobic activity, about eight hours of sleep, and less caffeine in the two weeks before a period are standard advice (Office on Women's Health).

If you start an SSRI, the first weeks can be uncomfortable. See our article on why SSRIs can make anxiety worse at first.

Supplements are less settled. The Office on Women's Health says vitamin B6 may help with PMS symptoms including anxiety. MedlinePlus says that in most studies vitamin B6, calcium, and magnesium are not helpful for PMDD (MedlinePlus). Our article on magnesium and anxiety covers that evidence.

What if you already have an anxiety disorder?

The research here is thin. A 2024 review describes treatment options for premenstrual exacerbation of anxiety disorders as limited and says psychosocial treatments are understudied. One small pilot study of variable sertraline dosing looked at depression, not anxiety (Lin et al., 2024).

Do not change your own dose around your cycle. Take your symptom record to your prescriber and ask whether the pattern changes the plan. Depression can flare in the same week, and our sister site depression.md explains how depression is recognized and treated.

When should you get help?

Contact a clinician if symptoms do not improve with self-care or if they interfere with daily life. MedlinePlus warns that suicide risk in women with depression is higher in the second half of the menstrual cycle (MedlinePlus). If you have thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988, or call 911. See also when to see a doctor for anxiety.

The bottom line

Anxiety that rises in the week before a period is a recognized pattern. Research links it to brain sensitivity to luteal-phase hormone changes. It may be PMS, PMDD, or a flare of an anxiety disorder that is present all month. One review found clear evidence of premenstrual worsening in panic disorder. Data for other anxiety disorders are limited. A daily record over at least two cycles separates the three. SSRIs, a drospirenone-containing pill, and cognitive behavioral therapy have the best evidence for PMDD.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can PMS cause panic attacks?

Panic attacks are listed by the Office on Women's Health as a symptom of PMDD, the severe form of PMS. A systematic review of prospective studies also found clear evidence that panic disorder symptoms worsen around menstruation. A daily symptom record helps show which one applies to you.

How long does premenstrual anxiety last?

PMS and PMDD symptoms start in the week or two before a period. The Office on Women's Health says PMDD symptoms usually go away two to three days after the period starts. Anxiety that continues through the rest of the month points to an anxiety disorder, not PMS or PMDD alone.

Is there a blood test for PMDD?

No. MedlinePlus states that no physical exam or lab test can diagnose PMDD. Diagnosis rests on symptoms rated day by day over at least two menstrual cycles, and a clinician may test thyroid function to rule out other causes.

Which medications are approved for PMDD?

The Office on Women's Health names three SSRIs approved by the FDA for PMDD: sertraline, fluoxetine, and paroxetine. It also names a birth control pill that contains drospirenone and ethinyl estradiol. A prescriber decides which one fits, because each has its own risks.

Does birth control help anxiety before a period?

One pill with drospirenone and ethinyl estradiol is indicated for PMDD symptoms in people who also want an oral contraceptive. Its label says it has not been evaluated for PMS, and effectiveness beyond three cycles has not been evaluated. A 2024 review says evidence for treating premenstrual flares of anxiety disorders is limited.
