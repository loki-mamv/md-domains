Why Does Anxiety Make It Feel Like There Is a Lump in My Throat?

October 20, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: The feeling of a lump in your throat when no lump is there has a name: globus sensation (globus pharyngeus). Anxiety and stress are often linked with it, and they can make you notice the feeling more. But the research does not show that anxiety is the only cause. Reflux and throat irritation also play a part. Globus is common and almost never dangerous, but some warning signs need a check by an ear, nose and throat (ENT) doctor.

What is the lump-in-the-throat feeling called?

Doctors call it globus. The Rome IV diagnostic criteria, used by gastroenterologists, define it as a persistent or intermittent, non-painful feeling of a lump or foreign body in the throat. For a formal diagnosis, all of these must be true:

  • No structural problem shows up on a physical exam, laryngoscopy (a camera look at the throat) or endoscopy.
  • The feeling happens between meals.
  • There is no trouble swallowing (dysphagia) and no pain with swallowing (odynophagia).
  • There is no gastric inlet patch, a small area of stomach-type lining in the upper esophagus.
  • There is no evidence that reflux or eosinophilic esophagitis causes the symptom.
  • There is no major esophageal motor disorder, such as achalasia.

The criteria also require symptoms at least once a week for the last 3 months, with onset at least 6 months before diagnosis. Many people describe it as a tightness, a pill that will not go down, or a ball sitting at the base of the neck. A telling detail: the feeling often eases or goes away while you eat.

Globus is not rare. In a 2016 survey of 3,006 adults in Guangzhou, China, 21.5% said they had felt it at some point in their life. Onset peaked between ages 35 and 54. Globus also accounts for about 4% of new referrals to ENT clinics, according to a 2003 randomized trial from the UK.

How is anxiety linked to globus?

The old name was "globus hystericus." A 2023 history commentary in Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery explains that doctors once saw it as a sign of hysteria or anxiety. Newer evidence points more to irritation and inflammation in the throat and esophagus. So the modern view is that anxiety is a strong partner, not the whole story.

What the studies show:

  • Anxiety is more common in people with globus. In the Guangzhou survey, anxiety affected 39.8% of people with globus versus 22.3% without it. Depression (31.2% vs 18.0%) and sleep problems (23.7% vs 13.6%) were also more common.
  • Less worry, less lump. A 2013 Japanese study followed 22 people with persistent globus after a normal camera exam. Their symptoms improved over about 9.6 months with no treatment. The drop in anxiety scores was the only factor tied to the improvement.
  • The link is not universal. A 1998 study of 88 referred patients in Austria found no more anxiety or depression than in general medical outpatients. Most of these patients had a pharyngeal or esophageal finding on testing, such as a motility problem or reflux.

Anxiety may work in two ways. It raises general body tension, and it turns your attention inward. Once you notice a throat sensation, worry about it can make it feel stronger. This is the same loop behind many physical symptoms of anxiety.

Is it caused by a tight throat muscle?

A popular idea is that stress clenches the muscle at the top of the esophagus (the upper esophageal sphincter). The evidence is weaker than the idea. A 1989 study in Digestive Diseases and Sciences measured that muscle during a stress task. Pressure rose under stress in both globus patients and healthy controls, and the rise was about the same. Resting pressure was normal in the globus group.

The authors suggested that people with globus may be more aware of normal changes in that muscle. In other words, the throat may not squeeze harder, but the brain may notice it more. Treatments aimed at throat and voice tension still seem to help some people (see below).

What else can cause a lump feeling in the throat?

A 2012 review in the World Journal of Gastroenterology notes that no single cause explains globus. It names gastroesophageal reflux as a major cause. Other things that can produce or feed the feeling include:

  • Reflux of stomach acid into the esophagus or throat.
  • Throat irritation, such as chronic pharyngitis or tonsillitis.
  • Esophageal motility problems, found in many of the Austrian patients.
  • A thyroid nodule or other neck mass, found in a few of the Austrian patients.
  • Neck arthritis (cervical spondylosis).

This is why a doctor looks for these causes before calling the feeling anxiety-related. If a thyroid swelling is a concern, thyroid.md covers nodules and goiter in more detail.

When is a lump in the throat a warning sign?

Globus itself is benign. A 2024 Laryngoscope study followed 377 people diagnosed with globus for at least 4 years. Four (1%) later developed head and neck cancer. The authors called this risk low and said it supports reassurance after a normal full exam.

Some symptoms do not fit globus and need prompt evaluation. The National Cancer Institute lists these possible signs of head and neck cancer:

  • A lump in the neck you can see or feel from the outside.
  • Trouble swallowing, or pain when you swallow.
  • A sore throat or a mouth sore that does not heal.
  • Hoarseness or a change in your voice.
  • Ear pain or trouble breathing.

Unplanned weight loss or food that sticks on the way down also calls for a visit. NCI notes that people who use tobacco are at higher risk. Our guide on when to see a doctor for anxiety covers other symptoms that deserve a check.

What treatments help globus?

Trials are small, but several approaches show benefit:

  • Reassurance after a normal exam. In the Japanese study, symptoms eased as anxiety fell after a clear endoscopy.
  • Speech and voice therapy. In the 2003 UK trial, 36 patients got either speech therapy or reassurance from a nurse. The speech therapy group improved much more after 3 months.
  • CBT-based education. A 2023 randomized trial in Thailand of 40 patients compared CBT-based psychoeducation, a neuromodulator drug, and the reflux drug omeprazole for 4 weeks. CBT and the neuromodulator both cut throat symptoms more than omeprazole. The neuromodulator caused more side effects.
  • Antidepressants for stubborn cases. A 2016 Chinese trial of 148 people with refractory globus found 71.7% responded to paroxetine over 6 weeks. That compared with 46.2% on amitriptyline and 14.0% on lansoprazole, a reflux drug.
  • Treating reflux when reflux is present. The 2012 review supports a trial of acid suppression for typical cases. The trials above suggest it helps less when reflux is not the driver.

Treating the anxiety itself also matters. Slow breathing, relaxation and other anxiety management techniques can lower the body tension and attention that keep the feeling going.

The bottom line

A lump-in-the-throat feeling with no real lump is called globus. It is common, affecting about 1 in 5 adults at some point in one large survey. Anxiety is more common in people with globus, and lower anxiety tracks with fewer symptoms. But reflux, throat irritation and esophageal problems also contribute. If the feeling comes with trouble or pain swallowing, a voice change, weight loss, or a lump you can feel in the neck, see a doctor or ENT. Otherwise, reassurance, speech therapy and treating anxiety all have evidence behind them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can anxiety make it feel hard to swallow?

Anxiety can make the throat feel tight, but true globus does not stop food from going down. In fact, the lump feeling often eases while eating. If food actually sticks or swallowing hurts, that is not typical globus and needs a medical exam.

How long does globus last?

It often comes and goes over months, and it can return. In one Japanese study, symptoms improved over about 9 to 10 months without treatment, mostly as anxiety eased. Treatments such as speech therapy or CBT-based education may speed this up.

Does drinking water help a lump in the throat from anxiety?

Many people find that swallowing food or liquid briefly eases globus, because the feeling tends to fade during meals. This does not treat the cause. Repeated dry swallowing to check the lump can keep attention on the throat and make it feel worse.

Should I see an ENT or a gastroenterologist for globus?

An ENT is the usual first stop because they can look at the throat with a small camera. If reflux, swallowing problems or esophageal symptoms are present, a gastroenterologist may do further tests. Your primary care doctor can help decide which to see first.

Is globus a sign of throat cancer?

Rarely. In a US study that followed 377 people with globus for at least 4 years, 1% developed head and neck cancer. A lump you can feel in the neck, pain, voice change, or trouble swallowing are the signs that need prompt checking.
